MEERA'S WRITING STYLE - SIMPLE GUIDE
====================================
23 September 2026


1. THE BIG IDEA
---------------
Meera writes like a calm scientist explaining something to a friend.
She is careful, honest and never tries to sell.

- Who she is: founder of Skinstinct, an Indian skincare brand. She worked in
  medicine-making (pharma) before this. She is not a skin doctor, and she says so.
- What we studied: 15 pieces she wrote: 4 LinkedIn posts and 11 email newsletters.
- Her one message: what's written on a skincare label is only the start of the
  story. The real answer is in how the product is made.
- Why people trust her: she admits her mistakes, says what she doesn't know, and
  doesn't push products. That makes her strong points believable.


2. HER 5-STEP RECIPE
--------------------
Almost every piece she writes follows the same 5 steps, in the same order.

  Real fact -> Explain why -> What I'm NOT saying -> Admit something -> Question for the reader

Step 1. Start with a real fact
   A number, a moment she saw, or something people wrongly believe.
   Her words: "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting..."

Step 2. Explain why
   Gives 2 or 3 reasons, one by one: "The first thing... The second thing..."
   Her words: "The second thing the percentage doesn't tell you..."

Step 3. Say what she's NOT claiming
   Stops readers from taking her point too far.
   Her words: "I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work."

Step 4. Admit something
   A mistake, a problem not solved yet, or "we don't sell this".
   Her words: "We don't currently sell a Vitamin C product."

Step 5. End with a question
   Something the reader can ask any brand.
   Her words: "You should ask for them. Not from us specifically."

Step 2 is the biggest part, about half the post. The other steps are short.


3. HER WRITING HABITS
---------------------
Length
   Every piece is between 340 and 540 words, usually about 450.
   That's roughly a 2-minute read.

Sentences: one long, then one short
   Her most recognisable habit is a long sentence followed by a very short,
   blunt one.
   - "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is also
     not helpful."
   - "The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was
     designed for the wrong context."

Words she uses
   - Real science words, explained right away. She says "pH" or "CoA" (a lab
     test report), then explains it. She doesn't dumb it down.
   - Exact numbers: "pH 5.5-5.8", "14 months", "67%". Never "a lot" or "many".
   - British spelling: colour, moisturiser, oxidise.
   - A dash with spaces ( - ) for side comments.

Phrases she repeats
   "I want to..."                    12 times
   "actually"                        11 times
   "honest"                           9 times
   "I'm not saying... I'm saying..."  5 times

Things she never does
   - No exclamation marks (zero in about 6,700 words)
   - No emoji or hashtags
   - No "best", "amazing" or "game-changing"
   - No attacking other brands by name
   - No "buy now" or discount offers


4. HOW SHE EARNS TRUST: 4 TRICKS
--------------------------------
Each trick works the same way: she gives something up first, so readers
believe her main point.

1. She stops you from taking her point too far.
   "I'm not saying the people in that room were incompetent. I'm saying the
   system had a gap." It makes her sound fair.
2. She admits her own mistakes.
   "It took us embarrassingly long to make the connection." Because she's hard
   on herself, her criticism of others feels honest.
3. She says when she has nothing to sell.
   "I'm not selling a sunscreen." Readers stop wondering if it's an ad.
4. She says how strong the proof is.
   Proof is "solid", "thinner" or "essentially absent". She even defends
   ingredients she doesn't use, like parabens.

She talks about her background only once, and modestly:
"I'm not a dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree." She shows her
knowledge through details instead of titles.

She says "I" much more than "we" (88 vs 24 times). "I" is for her opinions
and mistakes. "We" is for what the company did.


5. LINKEDIN VS NEWSLETTER
-------------------------
Her voice is the same in both. What changes is who she's talking to and how
she starts.

                      LINKEDIN                        NEWSLETTER
Who reads it          Other founders, smart shoppers  Her customers
How it starts         Straight into a fact or story   "Hi," then often "I want to talk about..."
How it ends           Advice anyone can use; no name  A promise or tip; signed "Meera"
Uses her own numbers  Often (2 of 4 posts)            Rarely (1 of 11)
Sentence length       Shorter, punchier               A bit longer

Her LinkedIn posts are her best work. They start faster and use more of her
own data.

One fix for LinkedIn: her paragraphs are long. LinkedIn only shows the first
2-3 lines before "see more", so shorter paragraphs will read better on phones.


6. KEY INSIGHTS
---------------
1. Her best posts use facts only she has. Her post about product returns in
   humid cities (like Mumbai and Chennai) and her story about the 2021 meeting
   work because no one else could write them.
2. She has more ideas than she has posted. 11 newsletters but only 4 LinkedIn
   posts. Each newsletter can become a post.
3. Her style is easy to copy. She uses the same 5 steps every time. A draft
   that follows them will sound like her.
4. India is her most unused topic. Only 1 of 15 pieces is about India, yet few
   brands talk honestly about Indian weather and rules.
5. Her readers trust her. 67% of customers buy again within a year. Most
   brands get 20-40%.
6. She never tells readers what to do next. No links to her test results or
   products, even when she says they're published. A soft link would help
   without feeling like selling.

Her 5 main topics (LinkedIn posts so far)
   Label vs reality   - The % on the label doesn't tell the full story      (1)
   Empty claims       - "Clinically tested", "natural", "clean" can mean anything (1)
   Founder honesty    - Mistakes, delays and why she built the brand        (1)
   India              - Products made for other climates don't suit India   (1)
   Her choices        - Why no fragrance, no parabens                       (0)


7. READY-TO-USE POST IDEAS
--------------------------
These 11 ideas come straight from her newsletters. At one post a week, that's
almost 3 months of content with no new research.

 1. Why we have no fragrance
    "I've explained why our products have no fragrance a few hundred times.
    Most customers think we forgot it."
 2. 14 months without a Vitamin C launch
    "I've been trying to make a Vitamin C serum for 14 months. We still haven't
    launched one."
 3. Ask any brand for its pH
    "A customer asked if our serum works with her Vitamin C. I could answer for
    ours. The other brand doesn't publish theirs."
 4. Parabens aren't the villain
    "We don't use parabens. Not because the evidence says they're harmful. It
    doesn't."
 5. Fake "cold-pressed"
    "I've seen 'cold-pressed' oils arrive with temperature records showing they
    weren't."
 6. "7 peptides" is just a label
    "Seven peptides at weak strength is not better than two at the right
    strength."
 7. You're using too little sunscreen
    "SPF ratings assume a thick layer. Most people apply half that or less."
 8. 0.1% can still be on the label
    "A product can contain 0.1% niacinamide and still list it."
 9. Ceramides at the bottom of the list
    "If ceramides come after the preservatives on the list, there's barely any
    in there."
10. 67% buy again, and what we got wrong
    "67% of our customers buy again. I'm more interested in what we got wrong."
11. Not a doctor, and that's fine
    "I'm not a dermatologist. I spent two years in pharma, and that shapes
    everything we make."

Start with 1, 2 and 10. They use her own experience, which is what makes her
best posts work.

More ideas

   - A Vitamin C diary: short updates on what's going wrong until it launches
   - Customer questions of the month, answered in her 5 steps
   - What Indian skincare labels do and don't have to prove


8. THINGS TO FIX BEFORE POSTING
-------------------------------
Habits to use less
   - Too many "I want to be careful / honest". Once is fine. Every post starts
     to sound like a template.
   - Slow starts in newsletters. "I want to talk about..." wastes the first
     line. Start with the fact instead.
   - The same ending again and again. "That's useful information" closes 3
     pieces.

Numbers that don't match (her brand is built on being accurate, so these matter)
   - Company age: "Three years later" vs "18 months of Skinstinct"
   - Humid-city returns: "71% of texture returns, then dropped to 8%" vs
     "return rate almost three times higher"
   - Product testing: "tested every 3 months for a year" vs "six-month" testing

Facts to double-check
   - The sunscreen study and the "SPF 30 becomes SPF 5-6" number.
   - That "clinically tested" needs no proof in India. India's advertising
     standards body (ASCI) does expect claims to be backed up.
   - The "20-40% of customers buy again" number for other brands.

Simple fix: keep a one-page fact sheet with all her key numbers, and check
every post against it.


9. HOW TO WRITE A POST LIKE HER
-------------------------------
Quick checklist
   [ ] First line has a number, a place, a date or a common wrong belief
   [ ] 2-3 reasons explained, one by one
   [ ] One "I'm not saying... I'm saying..."
   [ ] One honest admission
   [ ] Ends with a question readers can ask any brand
   [ ] About 400-450 words, short paragraphs
   [ ] No exclamation marks, emoji, hashtags or selling
   [ ] Every number matches the fact sheet


Example post (built only from her "natural" newsletter)
.......................................................

I've seen oil arrive at a manufacturer labelled "cold-pressed", with
temperature records showing it had been processed well above what
cold-pressing allows.

I don't know if that was a supplier mistake or something else. What I know is
that the paperwork didn't match the claim - and the claim would have reached a
product label if nobody had checked.

Cold-pressing matters. Pressing oil at low temperatures protects its natural
fats, so when the claim is true it means something. When it isn't, it means
nothing. The label looks the same either way.

This is the wider problem with "natural" in skincare. The word has no legal
definition in India. A brand can use it for almost any reason, or none at all.
This is not a loophole. It is how the rules work today.

"Plant-derived" has the same problem. Squalane can come from olives or
sugarcane. Both are plant-derived. They are very different in how they're
sourced and sometimes in how pure they are. You can't tell which one you're
getting from the label.

I'm not saying natural is better or worse than synthetic. Some synthetic
ingredients are safer and better studied. Some natural ones are more likely to
irritate skin.

I'm saying "natural" tells you about the marketing. It tells you nothing
reliable about the product.

I've had this conversation with our own manufacturer about fifteen times in
two years. It is slower than trusting the paperwork. It is also the only way
I've found to know what's in the bottle.

If you're building a skincare brand, ask your suppliers for processing
records. If you're buying skincare, ask the brand where its ingredients come
from. The ones who can answer clearly have been asking the right questions.
